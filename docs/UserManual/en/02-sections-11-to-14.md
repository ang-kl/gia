# Soleat User Manual — Sections 11 to 14

## 11. Languages, Local Food Names, and Search Meaning

Soleat supports multiple interface languages, but food search is not only a translation task.

Food names can change meaning across cuisines, countries, dialects, and local usage. A word such as “dumpling” may point to different foods depending on whether the user means Chinese dumplings, Japanese gyoza, Korean mandu, Polish pierogi, or Singapore-style dumpling soup.

Soleat’s food search should therefore use language as one clue, not the whole answer. A good search may also depend on cuisine, dish type, country, city, cooking method, ingredient, local name, native-script name, and the user’s location.

### 11.1 Interface language

Soleat may show menus, buttons, labels, and explanations in the supported interface language where available.

The English manual is the master copy. Translations should preserve meaning, especially for food terms, cuisine names, dish names, and local usage.

### 11.2 Food names and local meaning

Food terms should not be flattened into generic words.

For example:

```text
Chinese dumplings
Singapore dumpling soup
Japanese gyoza
Korean mandu
```

These may be related by broad food category, but they are not the same search intent.

### 11.3 Native-script and local food names

When supported, Soleat may use local-script or native-script names to help users recognise dishes more naturally.

This is useful when a dish is better known by its local name than by an English translation.

### 11.4 How to search more clearly

Be specific when a food word has many meanings.

Better:

```text
Chinese dumplings near Chinatown
Japanese gyoza near Orchard
Singapore dumpling soup near Bugis
Korean mandu near Tanjong Pagar
```

Less clear:

```text
dumpling
```

If results feel mixed up, add the cuisine, country, city, dish name, or cooking method.

## 12. Food Search Overview

Soleat can begin a food search from many starting points.

You do not need to know the restaurant name. You can start from what you feel like eating, what you want to learn, what you want to try, or where you need to be.

### 12.1 Search by cuisine

Use cuisine when you know the food style you want.

Examples:

```text
Japanese near Orchard
Thai near City Hall
Malay food near me
Vietnamese near Tanjong Pagar
```

Cuisine search is helpful when you want a broad but meaningful direction.

### 12.2 Search by dish

Use a dish name when you know what you want to eat.

Examples:

```text
ramen near Somerset
laksa near Katong
satay near Lau Pa Sat
claypot rice near Chinatown
```

Dish search is useful when cuisine alone is too broad.

### 12.3 Search by ingredient or cooking method

Use ingredient or cooking method when you know the kind of food you want but not the exact dish.

Examples:

```text
grilled fish near Bugis
fried noodles near City Hall
roasted duck near Chinatown
steamed dumplings near Tanjong Pagar
```

Cooking-method search helps when you are exploring what to try.

### 12.4 Search by set meal or practical meal need

Use this when you care about the eating situation more than the cuisine name.

Examples:

```text
set meal near Raffles Place
quick lunch near City Hall
solo dinner near Orchard
late supper near Bugis
```

This is useful when time, budget, opening hours, or convenience matters.

### 12.5 Search recognised venues and hidden gems

Use recognised search when you want curated or notable places.

Use hidden-gem search when you want discovery beyond ordinary high-visibility results.

These are useful when you want a better starting point than a generic map search.

### 12.6 Search with location context

Location changes the usefulness of results.

For example, “ramen” is broad. “ramen near Orchard MRT” is more useful. “ramen near Orchard MRT before 9 PM” is closer to a real decision.

When planning, combine food intent with location, opening hours, transport, bus, car park, or Sketchbook context.

## 13. Cuisine Search

Cuisine Search is the flagship food-discovery experience in Soleat.

Use it when you want to search by cuisine, dish, cooking method, local food picks, recognised sources, filters, or map context.

### 13.1 When to use Cuisine Search

Use Cuisine Search when you want more control than a simple chat search.

It is useful when you want to:

- choose one or more cuisines;
- explore a cuisine you do not know well;
- find what to try;
- learn about a dish before choosing it;
- search with rating or price filters;
- consider recognised venues;
- compare results on a map;
- copy useful eatery cards; or
- save candidates into Sketchbook.

### 13.2 Choose cuisine, dish, or food idea

You can begin with a cuisine, dish, ingredient, cooking method, or food idea.

Examples:

```text
Korean
Peranakan
ramen
banana leaf rice
grilled seafood
set meal
```

If you are unsure, start broad. You can narrow later.

### 13.3 Use filters carefully

Filters help refine results, but too many filters can remove useful places.

If the result list is weak, try removing one filter, broadening the cuisine, changing the location, or using free-text search.

### 13.4 Use “what to try” guidance

Cuisine Search may help you understand what dishes are worth trying within a cuisine or local food context.

This is useful when you want to discover something new but do not know the dish names yet.

### 13.5 Use recognised and curated sources

Where available, Cuisine Search may connect with recognised venues or curated food sources.

Use this when you want a more guided starting point, such as notable places, curated lists, or recognised food options.

### 13.6 Use map and practical context

Cuisine results become more useful when combined with real-world context.

Check whether a place is convenient by looking at location, opening hours, train context, Singapore Train Station Exits, Singapore Bus Information, Singapore Car Parks, weather, and map layers where available.

### 13.7 When results are weak

If results are too narrow, broaden the search.

Try:

```text
Korean
instead of Korean BBQ set meal near one small street
```

If results are too broad, add a stronger clue.

Try:

```text
Korean mandu near Tanjong Pagar
instead of dumpling
```

## 14. Eatery Cards

Eatery cards are the main way Soleat presents food-place results.

A card helps you compare places without jumping between many separate search results.

### 14.1 What an eatery card may show

Depending on the result and available data, an eatery card may include:

- place name;
- cuisine or food label;
- area or location;
- opening information;
- rating or crowd context;
- price range or overseas FX context where relevant;
- recognition badges where available;
- suggested dish or “what to try” line;
- map link or action buttons;
- transport, bus, car park, or station context where available; and
- copy or save actions.

### 14.2 Read the card as a decision aid

Do not read an eatery card only as a listing.

Use it to answer practical questions:

- Is this the kind of food I want?
- Is it open at a suitable time?
- Is it near where I am going?
- Is transport or parking practical?
- Is there a dish worth trying?
- Should I save this for later?

### 14.3 Copy one card or copy all

Use copy actions when you want to keep useful results.

Copied cards can go into Clipboard first, then be organised into Sketchbook cabinets and drawers for later planning.

### 14.4 Hide weak cards

Hide a card when it is not useful for the current decision.

This helps you focus on the better candidates instead of scrolling through the same weak options.

### 14.5 Compare before deciding

A good decision may require more than the best rating.

Compare cards by food fit, dish interest, opening hours, travel convenience, bus or train context, car park context, price, and whether the place fits the occasion.

### 14.6 Save for planning

If you are not deciding immediately, save the card.

Sketchbook is useful when you want to group eateries by trip, day, time slot, area, route, or event.
